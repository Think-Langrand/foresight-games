# Future of Public Health — Scenario Card Game (by-hand / offline version)

*A no-tech backup for the card game. If the site is down, run it straight off this
sheet and the printed uncertainty deck. Matches the digital game's content exactly.*

**The prompt:** *Build a future scenario from your cards.*

---

## How to run it (≈30–40 min)

1. **Form teams** — one per breakout room / table (aim for 3–6 people).
2. **Build a three-card world.** Each team picks **three uncertainties from three
   different dimensions** below, and for each one chooses **a single outcome** that
   they find most plausible or most interesting. (Deal them at random, or just cut
   teams loose to choose all three themselves — either works.)
   - Three different dimensions, one outcome each = the team's "world."
   - Outcomes tagged **(Edge)** are the darker / complicating futures; **(Wildcard)**
     is a stress-test. A team can include one for a richer world, but it's optional.
3. **Talk it through.** Read the three chosen conditions together:
   *What logic combines them? How does the field of public health show up and act in
   this world?* (~10 min of discussion.)
4. **Capture the scenario** using the questions in
   [Build your scenario](#build-your-scenario) — teams can jump around and answer in
   any order.
5. **Name the world** and, if time allows, each team shares their world with the room.

> A blank [team capture sheet](#team-capture-sheet) is at the bottom to copy/print.

## The uncertainties (13) — pick three, one outcome each

Grouped by the five capability domains. Each uncertainty has one question and four
possible outcomes.

### Permission to Act

**1. The shape of the public**
*What still holds people together enough to act on health collectively?*
- **Shared civic public** — People see health as a common responsibility and accept shared solutions.
- **Layered publics** — National consensus is weak, but local and identity-based communities coordinate internally.
- **Personalized publics** — People organize around individualized risks, values, and information feeds.
- **No common public (Edge)** — Health is treated mainly as a private responsibility and consumer choice.

**2. Public health's permission to act**
*How much permission does public health have to act?*
- **Broad permission to act** — Public health has durable civic support and clear legal authority.
- **Issue-by-issue permission** — Authority is accepted for some threats but contested for others.
- **Place-dependent permission** — Legitimacy and powers differ sharply across states and communities.
- **Constant political resistance (Edge)** — Public health remains a routine target and its powers are tightly constrained.

### Capacity to Act

**3. The institutional base**
*What kind of institutional base can public health sustain?*
- **Strong public backbone** — Core institutions have durable funding, staff, and infrastructure.
- **Lean core, shared capacity** — Small institutions rely on networks, pooled funds, and shared services.
- **Strong nodes, weak gaps** — Capable organizations coexist with major geographic and functional holes.
- **Hollowed and outsourced (Edge)** — Critical functions move to vendors or disappear as public capacity shrinks.

**4. The workforce model**
*Who does the work of public health?*
- **Rebuilt public workforce** — A broader multidisciplinary workforce is recruited and retained.
- **Small AI-augmented core** — Lean teams work through automation, CHWs, and shared specialists.
- **Distributed partner workforce** — Contractors, community organizations, universities, and creators do more of the work.
- **Chronic shortage and memory loss (Edge)** — Vacancies persist and capability resets with each departure.

**5. The threat environment**
*What kind of threat environment does public health face?*
- **Adaptation keeps pace** — Known climate and disease pressures grow, but investments contain many impacts.
- **Compounding known threats** — Heat, smoke, disasters, outbreaks, and displacement increasingly arrive together.
- **Slow-moving systemic crisis** — AMR, chronic disease, and environmental exposure create sustained strain.
- **Unfamiliar biological shock (Wildcard)** — A novel or engineered threat falls outside existing assumptions.

### Ability to See

**6. What public health can see**
*How visible is population health in real time?*
- **Live public health picture** — Integrated systems provide a trusted, real-time view of population health.
- **Connected local pictures** — Federated systems share enough data to coordinate while remaining decentralized.
- **Private platforms see more** — Companies have faster and richer data than public agencies.
- **Growing blind spots (Edge)** — Fragmented systems and off-grid care leave public health partially blind.

**7. AI infrastructure and access**
*Who controls and benefits from public-health AI?*
- **Public AI utility** — Trusted, governed tools are available across the field.
- **Shared network services** — Institutions pool AI capabilities, talent, and standards.
- **Vendor-owned stack** — Agencies rent closed tools and infrastructure from private firms.
- **Unequal AI arms race (Edge)** — Well-resourced actors accelerate while others fall further behind.

**8. Control of body data**
*Who owns and controls data from people's bodies?*
- **Trusted public use** — Strong governance allows biodata to support population health.
- **Individual control** — People decide who can use their data and may trade or license it.
- **Corporate control** — Platforms and data brokers hold the most valuable datasets.
- **Government access (Edge)** — Public agencies or law enforcement gain broad access for public or political purposes.

### Ability to Speak and Be Believed

**9. Where trust lives**
*Who do people trust to guide health decisions?*
- **Institutional recovery** — Public institutions regain trust through performance, transparency, and accountability.
- **Trust goes local** — Clinicians, community health workers, and local leaders become the trusted layer.
- **Trust moves to platforms** — People rely on brands, influencers, and personal AI for health guidance.
- **Identity-bound trust (Edge)** — People accept guidance mainly from sources aligned with their group.

**10. How health truth is established**
*How do people decide what health information is real?*
- **Verified evidence layer** — Authentication and provenance restore a broadly shared factual baseline.
- **Human-AI mediation** — Trusted people and supervised AI translate evidence for different audiences.
- **Competing evidence systems** — Communities use different experts, facts, and standards of proof.
- **Synthetic fog (Edge)** — False and real content become too difficult to distinguish at scale.

### Ability to Adapt

**11. The model of prevention**
*How does society try to stay healthy?*
- **Healthier conditions first** — Environments, food systems, housing, and social supports become the main intervention.
- **Medicine-led prevention** — Drugs, diagnostics, and clinical monitoring manage risk at scale.
- **Consumer optimization** — Wellness products, personal data, supplements, and biotech define prevention.
- **Personal responsibility regime (Edge)** — Health is moralized as an individual choice regardless of structural conditions.

**12. The structure of care**
*How connected is care outside hospitals to the public system?*
- **Distributed and connected** — Home, retail, community, and public systems share data and responsibility.
- **Platform-led but regulated** — Large platforms organize care under common rules and data standards.
- **Parallel systems with bridges** — Formal and alternative systems remain distinct but connect at key points.
- **Invisible shadow system (Edge)** — Large portions of care sit outside oversight, surveillance, and safeguards.

**13. Individual and movement power**
*Who can shape health behavior and policy at scale?*
- **Many local problem-solvers** — Individuals and small groups use new tools to create useful public goods.
- **Trusted super-connectors** — A small number of credible people become key partners and distribution nodes.
- **Influencers rival institutions** — Reach and identity matter as much as expertise or formal authority.
- **High-leverage disruption (Edge)** — One actor or small group can rapidly destabilize health behavior or systems.

## Build your scenario

Once a team has its three cards, work through these together. **You can go back and
forth — answer them in whatever order works.**

1. **Talk it through first** — What logic combines your three conditions? How does
   the field of public health show up and act in this world?
2. **Defining characteristics** — What two or three things are true across this whole world?
3. **Central theme** — What's the central theme of this scenario — if there is one?
4. **The new normal** — What has become normal here that would feel strange today?
5. **Broken assumption** — What assumption about public health no longer holds?
6. **In one sentence** — fill in the blanks:
   > Because **______** has developed in this direction, **______** becomes
   > increasingly common. This causes or enables **______**, which ultimately changes
   > **______** for public health.
7. **Name your world** — What would you call this world? (a short, evocative title)
