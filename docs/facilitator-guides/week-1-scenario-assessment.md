# Facilitator Guide
## Running the Week 1 Design Group breakout — Scenario Assessment
### NNPHI Foresight-to-Action Initiative · draft for steering committee

Week 1 is the first read. This is where a group decides how seriously to take the world it's been handed. Your job is to get them to read it honestly, name what's shaky without throwing it out, then step inside and leave with **six sharp changes** the later sessions can build on. You are not the expert on public health in the room. You are the one who keeps the room reading the same world, pushes the first answer into a specific one, and makes sure what gets said gets captured.

---

## Agenda — one anchor meeting

Roughly two hours. Times are from the top of the meeting. Each block maps to a tab in the worksheet.

| Time | Block | Tab | Lead |
|------|-------|-----|------|
| **0:00** | **Open, all.** The arc of the program, the purpose of today, what leaves the room. | — | Project lead |
| **0:15** | **Read-in & first reactions.** Read the scenario together. Capture gut reactions before analysis. | First reactions | Facilitator |
| **0:35** | **Assess the scenario.** Work the four questions, one at a time. | Assess the scenario | Facilitator |
| **0:55** | **Stepping into the future & our 6 key changes.** Brainstorm what's different, then narrow to six. | Stepping into the future | Facilitator |
| **1:30** | **Parking lot.** Sweep what got parked; name what carries forward. (Populated throughout.) | Parking lot | Facilitator |
| **1:35** | **Report back.** Each room: its six key changes and one open question. | — | Recorder |
| **1:50** | **Close.** What we do with it, what happens next session. | — | Project lead |

The intro is 15 minutes. The four tabs are the working block. Parking lot fills up the whole time — the 5-minute block is just when you sweep it.

---

## Your job, in four moves

1. **Read it in, then get out of the way.** Let honest first reactions land before anyone gets analytical. *"Before we pull it apart — what's your gut?"*
2. **Let them stress-test, then draw the line.** Assessing is for poking holes. Once you cross into the future, the world is a given. *"We logged the holes. For the next block, assume it holds and tell me what's different."*
3. **Push from "what's different" to "what's a key change."** A detail of the story isn't a key change until it changes the environment public health operates in. *"That's true in that world. So what does it change about how we operate?"*
4. **Sharpen, don't just collect.** Six vague changes are worth less than six specific ones. Capture in participant words, then tighten.

---

## Rules of the scenario

Read these aloud at the start, then leave them on the table.

- We are reading **one** future, not predicting the future. This is a world to think inside, not a forecast to grade.
- **First reactions before analysis.** Say the gut thing before the smart thing.
- **Poke holes now.** Assumptions, gaps, what's unclear — this is the session for it. Write them down; don't resolve them.
- **Then step inside.** Once we move to key changes, the world is a given. Whether it's likely is a different meeting.
- A **key change** is about the environment we operate in, not a feature of the story.
- **Specific beats vague.** Every change earns its place by being concrete.
- **Disagreement is recorded, not resolved.**

Week 1 is the one session where stress-testing the scenario is the assignment — but only in the "Assess" block. The moment you move to key changes, the door closes and the world is a given. Managing that pivot is the whole job.

---

## What a "key change" is

This is the move Week 1 turns on, the way the "so-what chain" is the move in Week 2.

A **key change** is a meaningful difference between today and this future that **changes the environment public health operates in**. Not a plot point of the scenario — a shift in the ground your institutes stand on.

A worked example, from a data-licensing world:

- **In the story:** people license access to their own health data.
- **Raw observation:** "data costs money now."
- **As a key change:** "Population-level datasets can no longer be assembled by default — every use requires negotiated, priced consent, so routine surveillance becomes a procurement problem."

Same idea, three levels of sharpness. You are pushing every change toward the third one.

The six you keep should each be:

- **Consequential** — it matters enough to shape decisions.
- **Distinct from today** — it couldn't be said about the present.
- **Important to public health** — it lands on institutes, not just on society at large.
- **Different from each other** — the six together span the scenario's breadth, not six flavors of one idea.

These six are the deliverable. Week 2 takes each of them and works the so-what chain. Sharp changes here make Week 2 possible; mushy ones stall it.

## The four assessment questions

The "Assess the scenario" tab works these four, one at a time.

1. **What feels most important or distinctive?**
   Push past "it's about data" to what's genuinely new. *"Distinctive compared to today — what couldn't happen now?"*
2. **What feels unclear, incomplete, or difficult to understand?**
   Capture confusion as data, not as grounds for rejection. *"Note it — this is exactly the kind of thing we flag upward."*
3. **What assumptions does the scenario appear to make?**
   Surface the load-bearing ones. *"What has to be true for this world to hold together?"*
4. **Is anything important missing that would need to be true for this future to make sense?**
   The gap-filling question. Watch for the group quietly rewriting the scenario here — capture the missing piece as an assumption, don't let them invent a new world.

---

## Stepping into the future

Read the prompt in and keep them in the present tense, speaking from inside the world:

> It is now several years into this future. What has become normal that would feel unusual today? What no longer works? What's become easier / harder? Who gained or lost influence? What do people now expect from institutions?

Brainstorm wide first — get every difference on the wall before narrowing. Then work the group down to **the six that most define this future**, testing each against the four criteria above. Cluster duplicates, cut overlaps so the six span the scenario rather than repeat it. Repetition reached by different paths is a signal worth noting, but the final six should be distinct.

---

## Vague versus specific

- **Vague:** "Public trust drops."
- **Specific:** "People stop sharing health data with institutes by default, because consent is now priced and brokered — participation has to be bought or earned."

Avoid vague verbs — *affects, impacts, changes, alters*. Name the specific thing that becomes true.

---

## The parking lot

Anything the group notices but should not solve today — implications, risks, opportunities, actions — goes to the parking lot. It runs the whole session. Your job is to keep the group from solving those items now: *"Good one. Park it. Later sessions are where that gets worked."* The later sessions really do pull from it, so nothing is lost by parking it.

---

## When it goes sideways

- **The group won't stop critiquing and won't step inside.** *"We logged the holes — they go upstream. For the next block, the world is a given. Tell me what's different in it."* Stay inside for the full key-changes block.
- **The group tries to rewrite the scenario.** Rewriting is out of scope. Note the objection, capture the missing piece as an assumption, and work the world as given.
- **The group lists features of the story, not changes to their environment.** *"That's true in the world. So what does it change about how public health operates?"*
- **The group can't get to six, or has twenty.** Cluster and cut against the four criteria. Six that span the scenario beat twelve that cluster.
- **First reactions turn into a debate.** Park it. Reactions are collected, not argued.

---

## How the worksheet works

The four tabs are non-linear — you can jump to any of them in any order. Everything is a card on a shared board: brainstorm areas are free sticky walls; the four assessment questions and "Our 6 key changes" accumulate individual answer cards, each tagged with its author. People add and edit their own cards live, together. The Recorder makes sure the six key changes and the open question are captured in participants' words before the week is locked.

---

## Report back

Each room brings, in participants' words:

- Its **six key changes**.
- **One open question** — something it flagged as unclear, assumed, or missing that should go upstream.

---

## Roles on the anchor dates

Everyone holds a role on every date.

- **Facilitator.** Runs one breakout for all four sessions. Same scenario each time, so the world gets deeper.
- **Recorder.** Fills the worksheet/site in participants' words, and submits.
- **Connector.** Watches across rooms for the changes that show up in several worlds. Two members per date.
